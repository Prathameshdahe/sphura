# Cotton, Steam and the Factory: Textiles in the Industrial Revolution

## Why textiles came first

The Industrial Revolution in Britain began in the textile industry, and above all in cotton. In the early eighteenth century most cloth was produced in homes under the putting-out system, in which merchants supplied raw fibre to families who spun and wove it for piece rates. Demand for light, washable cotton cloth was growing quickly, partly because Indian calicoes had made it fashionable, and the bottleneck was spinning: it took several spinners to keep one weaver supplied with yarn.

## The key inventions

John Kay's flying shuttle of 1733 doubled the speed of weaving and made the shortage of yarn worse. The response came in a series of spinning machines. James Hargreaves's spinning jenny, developed around 1764, allowed one worker to spin eight threads at once at first, and later more than a hundred. Richard Arkwright's water frame, patented in 1769, used rollers to produce a strong yarn suitable for the warp, but it needed more power than a person could provide and was therefore placed in mills beside rivers. Samuel Crompton's spinning mule of 1779 combined features of both machines and produced fine, strong yarn; by the 1790s it was the dominant spinning machine.

Weaving lagged behind. Edmund Cartwright patented a power loom in 1785, but it was unreliable, and hand-loom weavers remained numerous until the 1820s. Their incomes then collapsed as improved power looms spread, causing great hardship in weaving districts.

## The factory system

Arkwright's mill at Cromford in Derbyshire, opened in 1771, is often described as the first modern factory. It brought hundreds of workers under one roof, ran on a fixed schedule with two twelve-hour shifts, and imposed strict discipline with fines for lateness. Arkwright built housing for workers and recruited whole families, including many children, because their small hands were useful for mending broken threads.

## Steam power

Early mills depended on water power, which limited where they could be built. James Watt's improved steam engine, with its separate condenser patented in 1769 and its rotary motion developed in the 1780s, allowed mills to be placed in towns close to labour, coal and markets. Manchester grew from a market town into the centre of the cotton trade, earning the nickname "Cottonopolis". By 1830 cotton goods made up roughly half of the value of British exports.

## Raw cotton and slavery

The expansion of British mills depended on imported raw cotton. After Eli Whitney's cotton gin of 1793 made it far easier to separate seeds from short-staple cotton fibre, cotton cultivation spread rapidly across the southern United States, where it relied on the labour of enslaved people. By the 1850s the American South supplied most of the raw cotton used in British mills. The prosperity of the industry was therefore bound up with slavery, a connection that became a political crisis during the American Civil War, when the supply of cotton was cut off and tens of thousands of Lancashire mill workers lost their jobs in the "Cotton Famine".

## Effects on India

The rise of mechanised spinning in Britain reversed the earlier flow of trade. For centuries India had been the world's leading exporter of fine cotton textiles, but cheap machine-made yarn and cloth from Lancashire undercut hand spinners and weavers in Bengal and elsewhere. By the middle of the nineteenth century India had become a major exporter of raw cotton and an importer of British cloth, a reversal that later made hand-spun khadi a powerful symbol of the independence movement.

## Working conditions and reform

Factory work was long, repetitive and dangerous. Children as young as six worked in some mills. Public concern led to a series of Factory Acts. The Factory Act of 1833 banned the employment of children under nine in textile mills, limited the hours of children aged nine to thirteen, and, crucially, appointed paid inspectors to enforce the law. Later legislation, including the Ten Hours Act of 1847, reduced the working day for women and young people.
