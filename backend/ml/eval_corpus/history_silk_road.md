# The Silk Road: Trade Networks Across Eurasia

## A network, not a road

The term Silk Road was coined in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, but the routes it describes had been in use for well over a thousand years by then. There was never a single road. Instead, a shifting web of caravan tracks, river crossings, mountain passes and sea lanes connected China with Central Asia, India, Persia, the Arabian Peninsula, East Africa and the Mediterranean. Few merchants travelled the whole distance; goods usually passed through many hands, with each trader covering a stretch he knew well.

## Origins under the Han dynasty

The opening of regular overland trade is often dated to the missions of Zhang Qian, an envoy sent west by Emperor Wu of the Han dynasty around 138 BCE to seek allies against the Xiongnu confederation. Zhang Qian was captured and held for about ten years before he escaped and continued his journey. His reports about the kingdoms of Central Asia, including the powerful horses of the Ferghana valley, encouraged the Han court to extend its military garrisons westward along the Hexi Corridor.

## What was traded

Silk was the most famous export from China, prized in Rome to the point that the Senate attempted, unsuccessfully, to restrict its use. But silk was only one of many goods. China also exported lacquerware, paper and later porcelain. From the west came glassware, silver, wool and linen textiles, and horses. India contributed cotton cloth, pepper, precious stones and indigo, while Central Asian oases supplied grapes, melons and jade. Trade was usually conducted in a mixture of coins, bolts of silk used as currency, and barter.

## Cities of the routes

Oasis cities grew wealthy by providing water, food, lodging and markets. Samarkand and Bukhara in modern Uzbekistan became famous centres of commerce and learning. Dunhuang, at the western end of the Hexi Corridor, was a gateway into China; a sealed cave there, discovered in 1900, held tens of thousands of manuscripts in more than a dozen languages, dating from the fourth to the eleventh centuries. The Sogdians, an Iranian-speaking people centred on Samarkand, acted as the leading middlemen of overland trade from the fifth to the eighth centuries, and their language served as a commercial lingua franca.

## Exchange of ideas and diseases

The routes carried more than goods. Buddhism travelled from India through Central Asia into China, and later Nestorian Christianity, Manichaeism and Islam spread along the same paths. Technologies moved too: papermaking reached the Islamic world in the eighth century, traditionally after Chinese papermakers were captured at the Battle of Talas in 751, and from there it eventually spread to Europe. The routes also spread disease. Many historians believe that the plague that struck Europe in 1347, the Black Death, travelled along trade routes from Central Asia to the Black Sea port of Caffa and from there by ship to the Mediterranean.

## The maritime routes

The sea lanes of the Indian Ocean were just as important as the caravan tracks, and often more so for bulky goods. Sailors learned to use the monsoon winds, which blow from the southwest in summer and from the northeast in winter, to cross between Arabia, India and Southeast Asia on a predictable annual schedule. Ports such as Muziris on the Malabar coast, Calicut and Malacca grew rich on the trade in spices, textiles and ceramics.

## The Mongol peace and decline

In the thirteenth century the Mongol Empire unified most of the overland routes under a single authority. The resulting period of relative security, sometimes called the Pax Mongolica, made long-distance travel easier; the Venetian merchant Marco Polo's account of his journey to the court of Kublai Khan dates from this era. After the Mongol Empire fragmented in the fourteenth century, overland trade became more dangerous and expensive. From the late fifteenth century, European sailors opened direct sea routes to India and East Asia, and maritime trade gradually took over much of the commerce that had once moved by caravan.
