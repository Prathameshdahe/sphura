# A Practical Guide to Household Budgeting

## Why budget at all

A budget is not a punishment; it is a plan that tells your money where to go before the month begins. People who track their spending for even three months usually discover at least one category, often food delivery or unused subscriptions, where they spend far more than they had estimated. The goal of this guide is to build a simple system that takes less than thirty minutes a month to maintain.

## Step 1: Measure your real income

Start with take-home pay, the amount that actually reaches your bank account after tax, provident fund contributions and insurance deductions. If your income varies, as it does for freelancers and people paid on commission, use the average of your three lowest months from the past year rather than the overall average. Planning around a lean month means a good month becomes a bonus instead of a habit.

## Step 2: The 50/30/20 starting point

A widely used rule of thumb divides take-home pay into three parts: 50% for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings and debt repayment. Needs are expenses you cannot easily avoid, such as rent, groceries, utilities, insurance premiums and minimum loan payments. Wants include dining out, entertainment, travel and upgrades you could postpone. In expensive cities rent alone can exceed 40% of income, so treat the split as a starting point and adjust it rather than abandoning the budget.

## Step 3: Build an emergency fund first

Before investing for long-term goals, set aside an emergency fund that covers three to six months of essential expenses. Keep it in a liquid, low-risk place such as a savings account or a liquid fund, not in shares that may fall exactly when you need the money. Single-income households and people with irregular earnings should aim for the upper end of that range.

## Step 4: Track spending by category

Export your bank or card statement at the end of each month and group every transaction into a small number of categories: housing, groceries, transport, utilities, eating out, shopping, health, subscriptions and savings. Most people need fewer than twelve categories; more than that and the tracking becomes a chore. Compare each category with the previous month and with your plan.

Look out for small recurring charges. A music service, two video platforms, cloud storage and a fitness app can easily add up to more than ₹2,500 a month, and many households pay for at least one service they no longer use.

## Step 5: Deal with high-interest debt

Credit card balances that are not paid in full typically carry interest of 36% to 42% a year, far more than any investment is likely to earn. Paying off such debt is effectively a guaranteed return. Two common strategies are the avalanche method, which pays off the balance with the highest interest rate first, and the snowball method, which clears the smallest balance first for a psychological win. The avalanche method saves more money; the snowball method helps some people stay motivated.

## Step 6: Automate savings

Set up a standing instruction that moves your savings target to a separate account or a systematic investment plan on the day after your salary arrives. When saving happens automatically, you adapt your spending to what is left rather than saving whatever happens to remain at the end of the month, which is usually nothing.

## Step 7: Review every quarter

Once a quarter, compare your actual spending with your plan, check progress on the emergency fund and adjust for changes such as a rent increase or a new dependent. Annual expenses such as insurance renewals, school fees and festival spending should be divided by twelve and saved for monthly, so they do not arrive as a shock.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is making the budget so strict that it fails within weeks. Leave a modest amount for personal spending without needing to justify it. Another mistake is ignoring irregular expenses: a car service, a wedding gift or a laptop repair is not an emergency if you knew it would happen eventually. Finally, avoid comparing your budget with friends' lifestyles on social media; the only useful comparison is with your own plan from last month.

## A worked example

Consider a household with a take-home income of ₹90,000 a month. Following the starting split, ₹45,000 would go to needs, ₹27,000 to wants and ₹18,000 to savings. If rent is ₹32,000, needs will probably exceed their share, so the household might reduce wants to ₹20,000 and keep savings at ₹18,000 until the emergency fund is complete.
