# The Printing Press and the Spread of Knowledge in Early Modern Europe

## Before movable type

Before the middle of the fifteenth century, books in Europe were copied by hand, mostly by scribes working in monasteries and, increasingly, in commercial workshops attached to universities. A single copy of a long text could take months to produce, and errors accumulated with each generation of copies. Woodblock printing, in which an entire page was carved into a single block, was known in Europe by the early 1400s, but it was practical only for short texts and images such as playing cards and devotional prints. In East Asia, printing had a much longer history: movable type made of baked clay was described in China in the eleventh century, and metal movable type was used in Korea in the thirteenth century.

## Gutenberg's system

Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith from Mainz, developed a complete printing system around 1440 to 1450. His key contribution was not a single invention but the combination of several: a hand mould that allowed individual letters to be cast quickly and to identical height, a metal alloy of lead, tin and antimony that melted at a low temperature and hardened quickly, an oil-based ink that adhered to metal type, and a screw press adapted from the presses used to make wine and paper. The Gutenberg Bible, completed around 1455, was printed in about 180 copies, some on paper and some on vellum. Roughly 49 copies survive today, although not all of them are complete.

Gutenberg himself did not profit much from his work. A lawsuit brought by his financier, Johann Fust, in 1455 transferred much of the printing equipment to Fust and his partner Peter Schöffer, who went on to run a successful printing business.

## Rapid spread

Printing spread with remarkable speed. By 1500, printing presses were operating in more than 250 European towns, and historians estimate that between eight and twenty million books had been printed. These early printed books, produced before 1501, are known as incunabula. Venice became the most important printing centre of the late fifteenth century; the printer Aldus Manutius introduced compact octavo editions of classical texts and the italic typeface, making books more portable and affordable for students and scholars.

## Effects on religion and science

The printing press played a major role in the Protestant Reformation. Martin Luther's writings, including his German translation of the New Testament, were printed in hundreds of thousands of copies during his lifetime, and short pamphlets allowed arguments to reach readers across Germany within weeks. Printing also changed science. Because identical copies could be distributed widely, scholars in different cities could refer to the same page of the same edition, compare observations and correct errors in later editions. Detailed printed diagrams, such as those in Andreas Vesalius's anatomy textbook of 1543, could be reproduced accurately for the first time.

## Standardisation of language

Printers needed to sell books to as many readers as possible, so they tended to favour forms of language that would be understood across a wide region. Over time this helped to standardise spelling and grammar in vernacular languages such as English, German and French. William Caxton, who set up the first printing press in England at Westminster in 1476, had to choose between competing regional forms of English, and his choices influenced written English for generations.

## Limits of the revolution

The impact of printing should not be exaggerated. Literacy rates in the sixteenth century remained low, especially outside towns, and many people encountered printed material by hearing it read aloud. Books remained expensive relative to wages for most of the period, and censorship by both church and state authorities restricted what could be printed in many regions. Manuscript copying also continued for centuries, particularly for texts that were meant to circulate privately.

## Legacy

Historians still debate whether printing caused the great intellectual changes of the period or simply accelerated changes that were already under way. What is clear is that the press made texts cheaper, more numerous and more uniform than ever before, and that the habits it created, such as citing a page number, comparing editions and publishing corrections, became the foundations of modern scholarship.
