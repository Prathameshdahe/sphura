# Inside the Lithium-Ion Battery

## How it works

A lithium-ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions between two electrodes. During charging, ions leave the positive electrode, the cathode, travel through a liquid electrolyte and a porous separator, and slot between the layers of graphite in the negative electrode, the anode. During discharge the process reverses and electrons flow through the external circuit to power a device. Because the lithium stays in ionic form rather than being plated as metal, the design is far safer than the rechargeable lithium-metal batteries that preceded it.

## Cathode chemistries

The choice of cathode largely determines a cell's energy, cost and safety. Lithium cobalt oxide, used in the first commercial cells sold by Sony in 1991, offers high energy density and is still common in phones, but cobalt is expensive and its mining raises human-rights concerns. Nickel manganese cobalt (NMC) cathodes balance energy and cost and dominate long-range electric cars; manufacturers have steadily raised the nickel content, from NMC 111 to NMC 811, to reduce the amount of cobalt. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cathodes store less energy per kilogram but are cheaper, contain no nickel or cobalt, tolerate many more charge cycles and are much less prone to overheating. LFP has become the standard choice for stationary storage, buses and many entry-level electric cars.

## Measuring performance

Specific energy, measured in watt-hours per kilogram, tells you how much energy a cell stores for its weight. Typical NMC cells reach 250 to 300 Wh/kg, while LFP cells typically reach 160 to 200 Wh/kg. Cycle life is the number of full charge and discharge cycles before capacity falls to 80% of its original value; good LFP cells can exceed 4,000 cycles. The C-rate expresses charging or discharging speed relative to capacity: a 1C charge would fill a battery in one hour, and a 2C charge in thirty minutes.

## Why batteries age

Capacity fades for several reasons. A thin layer called the solid electrolyte interphase forms on the graphite anode during the first charges; it protects the anode but slowly grows, consuming lithium. Fast charging at low temperatures can cause lithium plating, in which metallic lithium deposits on the anode surface instead of entering the graphite, permanently reducing capacity and, in severe cases, creating sharp dendrites. Keeping a battery at 100% charge in a hot environment accelerates degradation, which is why many phones and cars now limit the maximum charge or delay full charging until just before it is needed.

## Thermal runaway

If a cell is damaged, overcharged or has an internal short circuit, it can heat up until the electrolyte and cathode begin to decompose, releasing more heat in a self-sustaining reaction called thermal runaway. Battery packs therefore include a battery management system that monitors the voltage and temperature of each cell, as well as physical barriers that stop a fire in one cell from spreading to its neighbours.

## Solid-state batteries

Solid-state batteries replace the flammable liquid electrolyte with a solid one, such as a ceramic or sulphide material. This could allow the use of a lithium-metal anode, raising specific energy towards 400 Wh/kg, and would improve safety. The main obstacles are manufacturing cost, keeping good contact between solid layers as they expand and contract, and preventing dendrites from growing through the solid electrolyte. Several carmakers have announced pilot production, but large-scale use is not expected before the end of the decade.

## Sodium-ion alternatives

Sodium-ion batteries work on the same principle but shuttle sodium ions instead of lithium ions. Sodium is abundant and cheap, and the cells can use aluminium rather than copper for the anode current collector. Their specific energy is lower, roughly comparable to early LFP cells, but they perform better in the cold, which makes them attractive for stationary storage and small city cars.

## Recycling

Recovering lithium, nickel, cobalt and copper from used batteries reduces the need for mining. Hydrometallurgical processes dissolve shredded cells in acid and precipitate the metals, recovering more than 95% of the cobalt and nickel. Direct recycling, which aims to restore cathode material without breaking it down into elements, could be cheaper but is still at an early stage. Before recycling, many packs from electric cars can serve a second life in stationary storage, where lower capacity matters less than price.
