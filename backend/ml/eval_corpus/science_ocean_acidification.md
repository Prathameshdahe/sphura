# Ocean Acidification: The Other Carbon Dioxide Problem

## What is happening

The ocean absorbs roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide that human activities release into the atmosphere each year. This uptake slows global warming, but it changes the chemistry of seawater. When carbon dioxide dissolves, it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions. More hydrogen ions mean a lower pH. Since the start of the industrial era, the average pH of surface seawater has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1. Because the pH scale is logarithmic, this apparently small change corresponds to an increase in acidity of roughly 30%.

The ocean is not becoming acidic in the strict sense, since its pH remains above 7, so scientists use the term acidification to describe the direction of change.

## The carbonate problem

The extra hydrogen ions combine with carbonate ions to form bicarbonate. As a result, the concentration of carbonate ions falls. Carbonate is one of the building blocks that corals, oysters, clams, sea urchins and many plankton use to make shells and skeletons of calcium carbonate. Scientists describe the availability of carbonate with a quantity called the saturation state. When the saturation state of aragonite, the form of calcium carbonate used by corals and many molluscs, drops below one, unprotected shells and skeletons begin to dissolve.

## Effects on marine life

Shell-building organisms are especially vulnerable in their early life stages. On the Pacific coast of the United States, oyster hatcheries suffered mass die-offs of larvae between 2005 and 2009. Investigations linked the losses to the upwelling of deep, carbon-rich water that was corrosive to the larvae's first shells. Hatcheries now monitor seawater chemistry continuously and add sodium carbonate to buffer the water in their tanks when conditions are poor.

Tropical coral reefs face acidification together with marine heatwaves. Lower carbonate availability slows reef growth, while bleaching kills the corals themselves; together they may cause many reefs to erode faster than they can grow later this century. Some organisms may benefit: seagrasses and certain algae can photosynthesise faster with more dissolved carbon dioxide.

Acidification also affects behaviour. Laboratory studies have reported that fish larvae raised in high-carbon-dioxide water have altered responses to the smell of predators, although later replication attempts found smaller or no effects, and the question remains debated.

## Measuring the change

The longest continuous record of ocean carbon chemistry comes from Station ALOHA, north of Oahu in Hawaii, where measurements began in 1988. The record shows dissolved carbon dioxide in surface water rising in step with the atmosphere, and pH declining by about 0.0016 units per year. Similar time series exist near Bermuda and the Canary Islands. Autonomous floats now measure pH throughout the upper 2,000 metres of the ocean, filling in gaps between ship surveys.

## Regional hot spots

Polar waters are particularly sensitive because cold water dissolves more carbon dioxide. Parts of the Arctic Ocean and the Southern Ocean are expected to become corrosive to aragonite for at least part of the year within the next few decades. Coastal waters can also experience much larger swings in pH than the open ocean because of river inputs, nutrient pollution and upwelling.

## Economic stakes

Shellfish farming, coral reef tourism and fisheries that depend on reef habitats support the livelihoods of hundreds of millions of people. Small island states are especially exposed, because reefs protect their coastlines from storm waves as well as supporting fishing and tourism. Estimates of the global economic cost of acidification vary widely, but most studies agree that losses would be concentrated in tropical countries with limited capacity to adapt.

## What can be done

The only lasting solution is to reduce carbon dioxide emissions. Local measures can buy time: protecting seagrass meadows and kelp forests, which take up carbon dioxide and can raise the pH of nearby water, and reducing nutrient pollution, which worsens acidity in coastal areas. Proposals to add alkaline minerals to the ocean to increase its capacity to absorb carbon dioxide, known as ocean alkalinity enhancement, are being tested in small field trials, but their environmental side effects are not yet well understood.
