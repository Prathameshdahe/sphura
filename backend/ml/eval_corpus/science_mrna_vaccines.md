# mRNA Vaccines: From Lab Curiosity to Global Rollout

## The basic idea

A conventional vaccine delivers an antigen, such as a weakened virus, an inactivated virus or a purified protein, so that the immune system learns to recognise it. An mRNA vaccine instead delivers instructions. Messenger RNA encoding a viral protein is packaged into lipid nanoparticles and injected into muscle. Cells near the injection site take up the particles, read the mRNA with their own ribosomes and produce the protein for a few days, after which the mRNA is broken down. The immune system then responds to the protein as it would to a real infection, producing antibodies and T cells.

The mRNA never enters the cell nucleus and cannot change the cell's DNA. It is a temporary message, which is one reason why mRNA was long considered too fragile to be a practical medicine.

## Solving the inflammation problem

Early experiments in the 1990s were discouraging: injected RNA triggered strong inflammation and was destroyed quickly. In 2005, Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman reported that replacing the nucleoside uridine with a modified version, pseudouridine, allowed synthetic mRNA to slip past the innate immune sensors that recognise foreign RNA. Later work found that N1-methyl-pseudouridine worked even better and increased protein production. Karikó and Weissman were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2023.

## Delivery with lipid nanoparticles

Naked mRNA is quickly degraded by enzymes and cannot cross cell membranes on its own. Lipid nanoparticles solve both problems. They are made of four components: an ionisable lipid that is neutral in the bloodstream but becomes positively charged inside the acidic endosome, helping the mRNA escape into the cytoplasm; a helper phospholipid; cholesterol, which stabilises the particle; and a polyethylene glycol lipid that controls particle size and prevents clumping. The development of ionisable lipids for gene-silencing drugs in the 2010s made this delivery system ready for vaccines.

## Speed of development

When the genetic sequence of the SARS-CoV-2 virus was published in January 2020, vaccine developers designed mRNA candidates encoding the spike protein within days. The spike was stabilised in its pre-fusion shape using two proline substitutions, a technique developed earlier for related coronaviruses. Large clinical trials reported efficacy of around 95% against symptomatic disease in late 2020, and the first authorisations followed in December 2020, less than a year after the sequence was released.

## Cold chain and storage

The first mRNA vaccines required very cold storage. One product had to be kept at between -80 °C and -60 °C for long-term storage, while another could be stored at about -20 °C. These requirements complicated distribution in regions without ultra-cold freezers. Improved formulations and freeze-dried versions under development aim to allow storage in ordinary refrigerators.

## Side effects

Common side effects include a sore arm, fatigue, headache and fever for a day or two, reflecting the immune response. A rare side effect, inflammation of the heart muscle called myocarditis, was observed mainly in young men after the second dose; most cases were mild and resolved with rest.

## Manufacturing

Unlike vaccines grown in eggs or cell cultures, mRNA is produced by an enzymatic reaction in a vessel. A DNA template is copied into RNA by a polymerase, a cap structure is added to the start of the molecule so that ribosomes recognise it, and the product is purified to remove fragments and double-stranded RNA that could cause inflammation. Because the process is the same for every target, a factory built for one vaccine can switch to another within weeks.

## Beyond COVID-19

Because changing the target only requires changing the mRNA sequence, the same manufacturing process can be reused for many diseases. Candidates under study include vaccines for influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, cytomegalovirus and personalised cancer vaccines that encode mutations found in a patient's own tumour. An mRNA vaccine for respiratory syncytial virus in older adults was approved in 2024.
