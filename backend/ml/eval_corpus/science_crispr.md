# CRISPR Gene Editing: How It Works and Where It Is Going

## A bacterial immune system

CRISPR began as a curiosity in bacterial genomes: clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats, stretches of repeated DNA separated by unique spacer sequences. In the 2000s researchers showed that the spacers match fragments of viruses that had previously infected the bacterium. The bacterium transcribes these spacers into short RNA molecules that guide CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins to matching viral DNA, which the Cas protein then cuts. In effect, the system is an adaptive immune memory written into the genome.

## Turning it into a tool

In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that the Cas9 protein from Streptococcus pyogenes could be programmed with a single synthetic guide RNA to cut any chosen DNA sequence in a test tube. Within months, several groups demonstrated editing in human and mouse cells. Doudna and Charpentier received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020 for the discovery.

The guide RNA contains a 20-nucleotide sequence that matches the target. Cas9 will only cut if the target is immediately followed by a short motif called the protospacer adjacent motif, or PAM; for the most widely used Cas9 the PAM is the sequence NGG. This requirement limits which sites can be targeted, and engineered Cas9 variants with relaxed PAM requirements have been developed to widen the range.

## How cells repair the cut

Cas9 makes a double-strand break. The cell repairs it in one of two main ways. Non-homologous end joining glues the ends back together but often inserts or deletes a few letters, which usually disrupts the gene; this is useful when the goal is to switch a gene off. Homology-directed repair copies a supplied DNA template into the break, allowing precise changes, but it is inefficient and works mainly in dividing cells.

## Beyond cutting: base and prime editing

Because double-strand breaks can cause unwanted rearrangements, newer tools avoid them. Base editors fuse a disabled Cas9 that only nicks one strand to an enzyme that chemically converts one DNA letter into another, for example C to T or A to G, without breaking the double helix. Prime editors go further: they pair a nicking Cas9 with a reverse transcriptase and a longer guide RNA that carries the desired edit, so that small insertions, deletions and all twelve kinds of single-letter substitutions can be written directly into the genome.

## First approved therapy

In late 2023, regulators in the United Kingdom and the United States approved the first CRISPR-based medicine, a treatment for sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent beta thalassaemia. Doctors collect a patient's blood stem cells, edit them outside the body to disable the BCL11A enhancer, which reactivates production of fetal haemoglobin, and then return the cells after chemotherapy has cleared space in the bone marrow. In clinical trials most treated patients with sickle cell disease were free of severe pain crises for at least a year afterwards. The treatment is expensive and demanding, which limits access in the countries where sickle cell disease is most common.

## Agriculture

Gene editing is also used in crops and livestock. Because many edits simply switch off a gene, the resulting plants can be indistinguishable from those produced by conventional breeding, and several countries regulate them more lightly than transgenic crops. Examples include mushrooms that do not brown when cut, high-yield rice varieties and wheat with reduced gluten content.

## Risks and ethics

Off-target edits, in which Cas9 cuts at sites that resemble the intended target, remain a concern, although high-fidelity enzymes and careful guide design have reduced them. Delivering editors into tissues inside the body is another major challenge; lipid nanoparticles work well for the liver but not yet for most other organs. Editing embryos so that changes are inherited raises deeper ethical questions. In 2018 a researcher in China announced the birth of twin girls whose embryos he had edited, a move that was widely condemned and led to his imprisonment. Most countries prohibit clinical germline editing.
