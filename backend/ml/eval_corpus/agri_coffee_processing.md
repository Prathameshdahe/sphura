# From Cherry to Bean: How Coffee Is Processed

## The coffee cherry

Coffee beans are the seeds of a fruit called the coffee cherry. Each cherry usually contains two seeds surrounded by layers of skin, sweet pulp, a sticky layer called mucilage and a thin protective husk known as the parchment. Before the seeds can be roasted, all of these layers must be removed and the beans must be dried from a moisture content of around 60% to between 10% and 12%. How this is done, the processing method, has a large influence on the flavour of the final cup.

## The washed process

In the washed, or wet, process, the cherries are first sorted in water tanks; unripe and damaged cherries float and are removed. A machine called a pulper then strips off the skin and most of the pulp. The beans, still covered in mucilage, are left to ferment in tanks for 12 to 72 hours, during which microbes break down the mucilage so it can be washed away. The clean beans in their parchment are then dried on patios or raised beds. Washed coffees tend to taste clean and bright, with clearly defined acidity, which is why the method is common in Colombia, Kenya and Central America.

## The natural process

The natural, or dry, process is the oldest method. Whole cherries are spread out in the sun to dry for three to six weeks, and must be turned regularly to prevent mould and uneven drying. Only when the fruit is fully dry is it removed by hulling. Because the seeds stay in contact with the fruit for so long, natural coffees often have heavy body and fruity flavours reminiscent of blueberry or strawberry. The method uses very little water, which makes it suited to dry regions such as parts of Ethiopia and Brazil, but it carries a higher risk of defects if drying is poorly controlled.

## Honey and pulped natural processes

Between these two extremes lie the honey processes, developed and named in Costa Rica. The skin is removed but some or all of the mucilage is left on the bean during drying. Producers describe the results by colour: white and yellow honey keep less mucilage and dry faster, while red and black honey keep more and are dried slowly in shade, producing sweeter, more complex coffees. Brazil's pulped natural process is a similar idea that developed independently.

## Experimental fermentation

In recent years, specialty producers have experimented with controlled fermentation. In anaerobic fermentation, cherries or pulped beans are sealed in tanks without oxygen for several days, often with the pressure released through a one-way valve. Carbonic maceration, borrowed from winemaking, ferments whole cherries in tanks filled with carbon dioxide. These methods can create intense flavours of tropical fruit, cinnamon or wine, and they have won many recent competitions, although some critics argue that they mask the character of the farm and variety.

## Drying and resting

Whatever the method, drying must be slow and even. Beans dried too fast can crack and lose flavour stability, while beans dried too slowly risk mould. Many producers use raised African beds, which allow air to circulate under the coffee. After drying, the beans are rested in their parchment for 30 to 60 days so that moisture evens out across the batch; this period is called reposo in Latin America.

## Milling and grading

Before export, the parchment is removed in a dry mill, and the green beans are sorted by size using screens, by density on gravity tables, and by colour using optical sorters or by hand. Specialty coffee is usually defined as coffee that scores 80 points or more on a 100-point scale used by trained tasters, called Q graders, and has very few defects in a 350-gram sample of green beans.

## Coffee in India

India grows both arabica and robusta, mostly in the hills of Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, where coffee is typically cultivated under a canopy of shade trees alongside pepper and cardamom. A distinctive Indian speciality is Monsooned Malabar: natural-processed beans are exposed to the humid monsoon winds in open warehouses on the west coast for several weeks, which makes them swell, turn pale gold and lose much of their acidity. The process recreates the flavour that beans acquired during long sea voyages to Europe in the age of sail.
